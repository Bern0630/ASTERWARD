> Data updated through September 17, 2026, 15:00 Asia/Taipei. This article examines second-order transmission and does not claim that AI end demand has reversed.

## First-Order Effect

The most visible effect of higher rates is lower long-duration equity valuation. The US ten-year Treasury yield closed near 5.002% on September 15 after reaching 5.041% intraday. After the Federal Reserve raised rates by 25 basis points on September 16, front-end rates and the dollar remained elevated. Investors therefore reduced the present value of distant cash flows and raised required returns for data centers, REITs, utilities and unprofitable technology.

The equity decline is only the first layer. Data-center construction, power access, substations, cooling, networks and GPUs all require payment before revenue begins. The more important second-order effect is how funding costs change whether a project can finish, commence leases on time and generate enough cash to service debt.

## Second-Order Transmission

```text
Risk-free rates and credit spreads rise
↓
Weighted average cost of capital rises
↓
Construction interest and refinancing costs increase
↓
Debt-service coverage falls
↓
Cap rates rise and asset values fall
↓
Loan-to-value rises mechanically
↓
Additional collateral, equity, delays or smaller projects
↓
Grid, equipment and supply-chain orders are deferred
```

This chain does not require weaker AI demand first. Even if tenants still need compute, borrowers may phase construction because interest coverage is inadequate. Banks can reduce loan-to-value, private lenders can raise spreads and prepayment protections, and equity investors can demand higher returns. Orders therefore show delay, resizing and renegotiation before outright cancellation.

## Why Chip Demand and Financing Stress Can Coexist

TSMC's August revenue rose 53.3% year on year, indicating strong advanced-node and AI-chip demand. Semiconductor suppliers usually receive procurement orders before data-center revenue begins, so chip revenue can lead project cash flow by several quarters. Hyperscalers can also absorb higher rates on their own balance sheets and sustain strategic capex.

Third-party data centers, special-purpose companies, GPU rental platforms, industrial property and utilities rely more heavily on external finance. If debt maturity, rate type and lease commencement are mismatched, credit pressure can appear while chip demand remains strong. This timing difference is precisely what markets can miss.

## How Rates Enter the Project Model

Project revenue depends on energized IT load, actual utilization, rent per megawatt or rack, power-cost pass-through and lease duration. Costs include land, buildings, grid connection, mechanical and electrical systems, cooling, networks, maintenance, insurance, taxes, power and interest. Higher rates alter at least four inputs simultaneously: construction interest, term-debt cost, cap rates and required equity returns.

With net operating income unchanged, a higher cap rate directly lowers asset value, which then raises loan-to-value. If credit agreements contain loan-to-value or debt-service-coverage covenants, a project may need additional capital without missing an operating payment. This valuation-induced credit event commonly precedes a collapse in end demand.

## Who Bears the Risk First

Developer equity is usually first because cost overruns and valuation declines consume the shareholder cushion. Equipment and bridge finance follow because maturities are short, rates are often floating and collateral depreciates quickly. Project loans and private credit come next, depending on tenant leases, take-or-pay commitments, parent guarantees and completion support.

Utilities and governments can also carry implicit risk. If the grid and generation capacity are built before data centers arrive, costs may be recovered through regulated assets, tariffs or fiscal spending. Suppliers absorb risk through receivables, inventory and customer concentration. A project delay can therefore appear in the cash-conversion cycles of transformers, cooling, construction, fiber and power equipment.

## Malaysia's Second-Order Exposure

Malaysia is a useful case study. MIDA cited an industry estimate of approximately 4.6 GW of planned or under-construction data-center capacity. Forty-nine electricity supply agreements carry approximately 7.1 GW of maximum demand phased over five to ten years, while confirmed actual load was approximately 710 MW in September 2025. These figures describe different stages and are not interchangeable.

Bank Negara Malaysia's OPR at 2.75% does not lock data-center funding costs. The ten-year Malaysian government yield was approximately 4.17% on September 15, while dollar finance remains sensitive to US rates. Imported equipment, foreign-currency debt, grid investment and tenant leases can use different currencies, making FX a second source of funding risk.

## Equity Versus Credit

Equity rewards orders first: contractors win projects, equipment suppliers sell transformers and cooling systems, utilities sign electricity agreements, and industrial land appreciates. Credit asks slower but stricter questions: who funds construction, when rent begins, who pays interest if energization is delayed, whether collateral retains value, and whether tenants can terminate.

Equities can therefore rise while credit terms deteriorate. If a company announces more capacity while lenders reduce loan-to-value, raise spreads or demand more parent support, order growth does not imply higher shareholder returns. Credit conditions are often the leading indicator of a capital-cycle turn.

## Weakest Structures

The weakest projects lack reliable power and pre-leasing, use short-dated floating-rate debt, and rely on high terminal-value assumptions. If GPUs and network equipment sit in the same special-purpose vehicle and borrow against the building's long life, the structure understates residual-value risk on short-lived equipment.

Another weak structure makes tenants and lenders circularly dependent. Tenant prepayments support construction; developers cite those prepayments as demand proof to obtain loans; suppliers then expand against the developer's order. A delayed payment by one participant forces the entire chain to revise cash-flow assumptions.

## What the Market May Be Missing

Markets often treat data centers as an extension of AI chip demand and focus on GPU shipments and cloud capex. The second-order risk is that demand remains present but cannot be delivered at the original time, place and capital structure because funding costs are too high. The result is not the disappearance of data centers. It is migration toward stronger owners, phased construction and more minimum-payment obligations for tenants.

Higher rates can also increase industry concentration. Large hyperscalers and well-funded infrastructure investors can absorb higher costs, while weaker developers sell land, power rights or partially completed assets. Industry demand can continue to grow even as asset ownership is redistributed.

## Next Confirmation

Track project-loan spreads, loan-to-value, debt-service coverage, fixed versus floating debt, maturity walls, tenant prepayments, parent guarantees, lease-commencement conditions and construction delays. Market indicators include US and Malaysian ten-year yields, USD/MYR, REIT cap rates, and receivables at data-center developers and equipment suppliers.

The cycle remains healthy if announced, financed, power-secured, pre-leased, energized and utilized capacity rise together. If announced capacity keeps growing while financing spreads, delays, receivables and refinancing needs also rise, second-order effects have begun to dominate returns.
