As September drew to a close, I wanted to stop and review how the market had performed over the first three quarters. AI had run through much of the year's technology trade, but after nine months I wanted to know how far that trend had actually progressed.

Earlier in the year, the market focused on how much companies were willing to invest and how many orders reached the supply chain. By the fourth quarter, the questions had moved further down the chain. Was the equipment producing real revenue? Were supplier sales and margins rising with demand? Could the investment eventually generate enough cash?

I therefore looked at U.S. corporate capital spending alongside Taiwan's exports, TSMC's results, and the performance of its wider supply chain. The first three quarters produced real evidence of growth, but inflation, interest rates, and investment payback were again shaping valuations. This article is both a review of the first nine months and a fresh look at where the AI cycle could go next.

> Data in this article is current through 3:54 a.m. on October 16, 2026, Taiwan time. The review period covers January through September 2026 and includes financial results and market changes reported early in Q4.
>
> U.S. trading on October 15 had not closed at the cutoff time, so intraday moves are not treated as closing results. Third-quarter GDP for the United States and Taiwan had not been released; forecasts are not presented as actual results.

## The One-Minute View

1. **The first three quarters produced measurable growth.** U.S. private demand remained resilient, while Taiwan's GDP, exports, and large technology-company profits rose sharply.
2. **Stock prices already reflect substantial expectations.** Taiwan's weighted index is also heavily concentrated in large-cap companies, so a strong index does not mean every industry or investor earned the same return.
3. **Q4 puts more weight on the cost and payback of investment.** Orders still matter, but interest rates, financing costs, margins, and free cash flow increasingly determine the price investors will pay.
4. **U.S. customers and Taiwanese suppliers must be tested together.** Customer investment, revenue, and cash flow should align with supplier orders, shipments, margins, and collections before the growth chain can be considered complete.

## The First Three Quarters Were About Growth

### United States: Demand remains resilient, but inflation limits policy flexibility

U.S. real GDP grew at annualized quarterly rates of 2.5% in Q1 and 2.2% in Q2. Real final sales to private domestic purchasers rose 4.6% in Q2. Because this measure consists of consumer spending and private fixed investment, it is closer to final private demand than GDP that also includes inventories and government spending.[1]

Higher funding costs did not immediately stall the economy. Consumption, information equipment, and data-center construction continued to support business demand.

The pressure came from prices. Headline CPI inflation rose from 3.4% in August to 3.7% in September, while core CPI was 2.6%. Energy accounted for a meaningful part of the increase, but overall inflation remained above the Fed's objective.[2]

In September, the Fed raised its policy range by 25 basis points to 3.75%–4.00%. Its statement described economic activity as solid and private spending as resilient, while also noting that inflation remained elevated.[3]

That leaves two forces at work in Q4. Investment and demand still support growth, but inflation and funding costs raise the return hurdle that new projects must clear.

The labor market looks softer, not broken. Nonfarm payrolls increased by 29,000 in September and unemployment stood at 4.2%. Real average hourly earnings were 0.7% lower than a year earlier.[4][5] Business investment and output can grow without immediately improving household purchasing power.

### Taiwan: Exports and GDP are strong, but growth remains concentrated

Taiwan's real GDP increased 15.43% year over year in Q1 and 12.93% in Q2, bringing first-half growth to 14.15%. These are year-over-year rates and should not be compared directly with annualized quarter-over-quarter U.S. figures.[6]

Exports increased 44.6% year over year in Q3 and 46.2% over the first nine months. September exports rose 60.9% to US$87.22 billion.[7]

AI, high-performance computing, electronic-component upgrades, product launches, and pricing were the main drivers. Non-electronics and non-ICT exports also increased 25.4% in September. That hints at some improvement beyond technology, but one month is not enough to establish a broad recovery.

Higher export value does not mean physical output, corporate profit, and labor income rose at the same rate. Pricing, product mix, imported inputs, exchange rates, and production costs all affect the value ultimately retained in Taiwan.

## Markets Performed Well, and Prices Now Carry More Expectations

| Index | Price return, first nine months of 2026 |
|---|---:|
| Taiwan Weighted Index | About 65.5% |
| Nasdaq Composite | About 15.6% |
| S&P 500 | About 11.8% |
| Dow Jones Industrial Average | About 5.9% |

The Taiwan Weighted Index climbed from 28,963.60 at the end of 2025 to 47,940.13 on September 30, 2026, a gain of roughly 65.5%.[8][9]

That is a powerful return, but it is not the average experience of the full market. Large-cap stocks exert an outsized influence on the index, and their prices reflect both reported earnings and expectations for future demand, margins, and capital spending.

The relevant Q4 question is:

**Can actual earnings and company guidance continue to support the expectations already embedded in prices?**

Holiday spending, product launches, and year-end portfolio positioning often support Q4 activity, but seasonality is not a probability forecast for this year's market. Apple also notes that holiday demand usually makes its fiscal first-quarter sales higher, while product launches affect when distribution channels build new inventory and clear old models.[10]

Supplier restocking, factory production, and consumer purchases do not necessarily occur in the same quarter. More supplier shipments show that goods have moved to the next stage, not that final demand has already been realized.

## Q4 Shifts the Focus Toward Investment Returns

During the first three quarters, markets focused on the scale of AI investment and the orders reaching the supply chain. In Q4, the evidence chain needs to extend further:

**Orders → shipments → customer use → revenue and collections → margins and cash flow → investment payback.**

A delay at any stage can lengthen the return period.

### TSMC shows that part of the demand has become profit

TSMC reported third-quarter revenue of NT$1.494 trillion, up 50.9% year over year. Net income attributable to shareholders reached NT$763.1 billion, up 68.7%, and gross margin was 66.4%.[11]

For TSMC, advanced-node demand is no longer just an order forecast or industry projection. It has become reported revenue and profit.

The company guided to fourth-quarter revenue of US$52.3 billion to US$54.3 billion, above Q3's US$46.72 billion. That supports continued growth, but guidance is still a forecast. Actual results will depend on shipments, foreign exchange, early production costs for advanced nodes, and customer demand.

Gross margin remained high but fell 1.3 percentage points from Q2, mainly because of initial N2 dilution. Revenue growth, technology investment, and margins do not have to improve at the same time.

### New themes require separate tests for technology and financial contribution

Optical networking, CPO, advanced materials, and data-center power may all benefit from AI infrastructure. Companies within those themes, however, can be at very different commercial stages.

| Stage | Evidence that counts | What it does not yet prove |
|---|---|---|
| Development | The company has committed R&D or equipment | The product meets customer requirements |
| Qualification | Customer testing or qualification is complete | Shipment timing or order size |
| Production | Capacity is installed and shipments have begun | Margins and orders will persist |
| Realized | Related revenue, profit, and cash flow appear in results | Growth will be durable |

Largan has said that a fiber-array product passed qualification with one customer and that it expects capacity to be in place by the end of Q2 2027. It targets the second half of 2027 for volume production of its fiber-array unit.[12]

Those are concrete qualification and capacity milestones. They are not evidence of a large 2026 revenue contribution. Glass materials, advanced packaging, and 800V power architectures should be judged the same way: demonstrations, joint development, qualification, production shipments, and financial contribution need separate records.

An industry direction can be valid while an individual company's revenue and profit remain unproven.

## The Mid-October Correction Exposed Valuation Sensitivity

After September CPI was released on October 14, markets reassessed rate risk, AI-infrastructure financing costs, and investment returns.

The S&P 500 fell about 1.1% and the Nasdaq about 1.9% that day, with AI and semiconductor shares among the main sources of pressure.[13]

On the following day, the Taiwan Weighted Index fell 1,655.96 points, or 3.41%, to 46,969.87.[14]

| Investor group | Net trading on October 15 |
|---|---:|
| Foreign and mainland Chinese investors | NT$302.555 billion net sold |
| Investment trusts | NT$28.303 billion net bought |
| Dealers | NT$95.824 billion net sold |
| Total | NT$370.076 billion net sold |

The three institutional groups were not all sellers. A meaningful share of dealer selling came from hedging and should not be read entirely as a bearish directional view. Foreign net selling also does not mean that the same amount of capital immediately left Taiwan.[15]

**A one-day selloff does not prove that demand has reversed. It does, however, reveal how sensitive valuations have become.**

When inflation and funding costs exceed expectations, the market tends to mark down businesses with high valuations, long payback periods, or heavy dependence on financing first.

U.S. trading on October 15 had not closed at the article's cutoff. Relative intraday strength in health care and consumer staples was an early defensive signal, but not enough to establish a durable rotation.

## Put U.S. Customers and Taiwanese Suppliers on the Same Page

American companies are major funders and users of AI infrastructure. Taiwanese companies provide chips, servers, networking equipment, and components. Looking at one side alone can turn an order into assumed revenue or capital spending into assumed returns.

| U.S. customer side | Taiwan supplier side | Link to test |
|---|---|---|
| Capital spending and equipment purchases | Orders and shipments | Does customer spending lead to actual shipments rather than long-term commitments alone? |
| Cloud and AI service revenue | Advanced-node and server demand | Does installed equipment generate use and recurring revenue? |
| Operating and free cash flow | Gross margin and operating cash flow | Can both sides preserve financial quality while expanding? |
| Financing costs and credit conditions | Customer concentration and receivables | Does funding pressure delay projects, orders, or collections? |
| Investment and depreciation payback | Supplier expansion spending | Can new capacity earn a return within a reasonable period? |

If U.S. customer revenue, utilization, and cash flow improve while Taiwanese suppliers maintain orders, shipments, and margins, the correction is more likely to reflect valuation and funding costs.

If customers postpone capital spending, financing deteriorates, and suppliers cut guidance or build inventory at the same time, the decline can no longer be dismissed as market sentiment alone.

## Three Scenarios for Q4

| Scenario | Evidence required | Possible market response |
|---|---|---|
| Base | Orders and earnings hold, but rate pressure remains | Rotation between growth and defensives; wider company dispersion |
| Stronger | Customer revenue and cash flow improve alongside supplier guidance | Renewed support for technology stocks and broader participation |
| Weaker | Customers delay investment, suppliers cut outlooks, and financing worsens | A valuation correction spreads into earnings expectations |

### Base case: Growth continues, but performance diverges

Orders and profits keep growing while inflation and interest rates limit valuation expansion. Companies with reported revenue and cash flow have more support. Businesses still in development or qualification, or those heavily dependent on financing, experience greater volatility. This is the best-supported scenario today.

### Stronger case: Revenue and cash flow catch up with investment

U.S. customer revenue from AI, cloud, and data centers accelerates, capital-spending guidance holds, and free cash flow improves. At the same time, Taiwanese suppliers raise revenue, margin, and order expectations. That combination could allow gains to spread from large-cap leaders into equipment, materials, and other suppliers.

### Weaker case: Funding pressure becomes a demand and earnings problem

Inflation keeps rates high, customers delay projects, financing terms worsen, and suppliers report deferred orders, rising inventory, or falling margins. At that point, the correction would extend from valuation into earnings expectations, requiring lower growth assumptions.

## The Data Worth Tracking Next

### United States

- Headline and core CPI, and the Fed's policy direction;
- Treasury yields and corporate financing costs;
- Capital spending, AI revenue, and free cash flow at large cloud companies;
- Data-center utilization and receivables;
- Revisions to technology-sector earnings estimates.

### Taiwan

- Exports, export orders, and electronic-component shipments;
- TSMC revenue, gross margin, and advanced-node production costs;
- Inventory and customer concentration at server, optical, and materials suppliers;
- Whether foreign spot flows, futures positioning, and the New Taiwan dollar weaken together;
- Whether supplier expansion spending begins to produce operating cash flow.

The same sequence also works for longer-term industry research:

**Product development → customer qualification → commercial shipment → recurring revenue → profit and cash flow.**

Each stage needs its own evidence. Industry potential cannot substitute for company results.

## Conclusion

U.S. business investment and Taiwan's supply chain both produced verifiable growth through the first three quarters of 2026. Taiwan's exports, GDP, and TSMC's results show that part of the spending on AI and data centers has already become revenue and profit.

Stock prices, however, rose first. The Q4 question has moved beyond order volume. Investors now need to see whether customers can turn equipment into revenue, whether suppliers can defend margins, and whether cash flow can cover interest, depreciation, and the next round of investment.

**Growth remains intact, but growth alone is no longer enough to support every valuation.** I will be watching which companies can turn demand into profit and cash flow without weakening their balance sheets.
