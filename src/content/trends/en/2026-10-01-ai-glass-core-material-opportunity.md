While researching the AI supply chain, I found myself paying more attention to glass, specialty chemical, and ceramic companies.

I began with a simple question after seeing chipmakers investigate glass-core substrates: what is wrong with existing materials? Is supply insufficient, is performance reaching a limit, or is glass simply cheaper? The deeper I looked, the clearer the underlying issue became. **As more AI processors and memory stacks are placed in a larger package, the materials and processes beneath them must advance as well.**

That shift could create new business for companies not usually classified as part of the AI supply chain. My conclusion, however, is not that glass is about to replace every organic substrate, or that any company announcing work on a new material automatically becomes an AI beneficiary.

The useful questions are more demanding: **whose technology is actually adopted, whether production begins on schedule, and whether shipments can generate financially meaningful profit.**

## Stronger AI Chips Require the Substrate to Keep Up

AI-driven semiconductor demand has two distinct components.

The first is volume. Cloud providers and data centers need more GPUs, CPUs, networking chips, and high-bandwidth memory. That requires additional capacity in wafer fabrication, packaging and testing, and system assembly.

The second is technical improvement within each product. To raise computing performance, manufacturers place more compute dies and memory stacks inside the same package. Package area grows, routing becomes denser, and more data must move between dies.

Manufacturing difficulty does not rise in a simple straight line. Large substrates can warp during heating, cooling, and processing. Alignment tolerances for traces and vias also become tighter. Poor flatness, dimensional instability, or inconsistent metallization can reduce yield, signal integrity, and long-term reliability.

This is why research into new materials is not merely about producing more units. It is also preparation for the manufacturing requirements of the next generation of large, high-density packages. Intel's public glass-core program emphasizes lower warpage, dimensional stability, and denser interconnects. TSMC is addressing the same broader integration challenge through larger CoWoS formats, SoIC stacking, and co-packaged optics.[1][7]

## Why Consider Glass?

Today's advanced package substrates generally use organic cores and resin-based build-up structures. These materials benefit from a mature supply chain, but larger packages make warpage, dimensional change, and fine-line alignment more difficult to control.

Glass is attracting attention because of its flatness, dimensional stability, and tunable coefficient of thermal expansion. Those properties could support larger package areas and denser interconnects. Through-glass vias can also establish electrical connections between the two sides of the core.

Glass, however, introduces its own manufacturing problems:

- forming consistent through-glass vias with high aspect ratios;
- bonding metal reliably to a chemically inert glass surface;
- filling vias with copper without voids;
- cutting, handling, and inspecting large glass panels without cracks;
- reaching acceptable yield and cost after meeting the performance target.

Glass core substrates therefore should not be reduced to a story in which an old material fails and glass simply replaces it. A low raw-material price also does not guarantee a low finished-product cost. Customers ultimately compare performance, yield, reliability, and total process cost.[1][8]

### Similar Terms That Describe Different Things

Several uses of the word “glass” need to be separated:

- **Glass-core substrate:** glass remains as the permanent supporting core inside the package substrate.
- **Glass carrier:** glass temporarily supports dies during panel-level packaging and may be removed later.
- **Glass fiber cloth:** a reinforcement used in organic substrates and copper-clad laminates; it is not a glass core.

ABF is not another name for a glass core either. ABF is an insulating material used in build-up layers. Replacing the core with glass does not necessarily eliminate ABF or every organic material; the answer depends on the package structure.[9][10]

This distinction matters when evaluating companies. Producing glass fiber cloth does not demonstrate glass-core capability. Expertise in large display-glass processing does not prove that a company has passed semiconductor customer qualification.

## Glass Is Not the Only Route

Glass is one possible response to the constraints of larger packages.

Improved resins and new insulating films may extend existing process architectures. Asahi Kasei has developed a photosensitive polyimide film for panel-level packaging, targeting redistribution and multilayer insulating structures. At the time of the announcement, the material remained under customer evaluation.[3]

Ceramics offer high rigidity, heat resistance, and dimensional stability. Kyocera has announced the commercialization of a multilayer ceramic core substrate intended to reduce warpage and support high-density routing in large packages.[4]

Panel-level packaging, 3D stacking, and co-packaged optics address manufacturing scale, vertical integration, and data transmission respectively. They may coexist with glass cores in the same system, or create competing implementation paths.

The useful question is therefore not whether glass will replace every other material. I would instead ask:

1. Which product categories genuinely need a glass core?
2. How far can existing organic materials continue to improve?
3. In which premium applications does ceramic have an advantage?
4. When will the yield, cost, and supply capacity of each route become acceptable to customers?

## Some Materials Companies May Gain a New Line of Business

If glass cores and other advanced package structures gradually enter volume production, demand will extend beyond the glass suppliers themselves.

Potential beneficiaries may include:

- semiconductor-grade specialty glass and ceramic materials;
- photosensitive dielectric films, adhesion promoters, and cleaning chemicals;
- through-glass-via formation, electroless copper, and via-filling chemistry;
- cutting, drilling, handling, and inspection equipment for large substrates;
- redistribution-layer integration and reliability-testing services.

This is why companies such as Asahi Kasei, Kyocera, Corning, DNP, MKS/Atotech, and AUO have become relevant to my research. They occupy different positions in the value chain and should not be evaluated using the same evidence.

### AUO and Corning: From Demonstration Toward Process Validation

AUO and Corning have jointly demonstrated glass-core substrate technology. Corning supplies semiconductor-grade glass, while AUO contributes large-format glass processing, redistribution-layer, and metallization capabilities. AUO also says it is advancing through-glass-via formation, via metallization, and reliability validation.[5]

This is more substantial than a conceptual proposal, but the available evidence mainly demonstrates joint development and exhibition capability. It does not yet establish high-volume orders. The next evidence should be customer samples, qualification, dedicated production capacity, and revenue.

### MKS/Atotech: Solving the Metallization Problem

Atotech, a strategic brand of MKS, has introduced an integrated wet-metallization flow that addresses glass adhesion, electroless copper seeding, and through-glass-via filling. Its position in the chain is not glass production; it is enabling glass to enter a manufacturable circuit process.[8]

Process suppliers may benefit from pilot lines and capacity construction before the material itself reaches broad end-market adoption. Yet placement of equipment or chemistry in a pilot line still does not prove that end customers have committed to glass cores at scale. Customer count, recurring chemistry revenue, and the contribution to MKS's overall business remain important.

### Asahi Kasei and Kyocera: Extending Different Materials Routes

Asahi Kasei's new photosensitive film remains under customer evaluation, placing it closer to the sample and qualification stage. Kyocera uses the language of commercialization for its multilayer ceramic core, but shipment scale and customer adoption still need to be confirmed separately.[3][4]

These cases are a reminder that the advanced-packaging materials opportunity is not limited to glass. Insulating films, ceramics, and process materials may gain demand from larger packages even if glass-core adoption takes longer than expected.

## “Working on It” Is Not the Same as “Making Money From It”

Technology news often encourages investors to skip the middle of the process and count an R&D result as future earnings. Commercialization normally proceeds through several separate stages:

**Technology demonstration → Samples → Customer qualification → Initial purchases → Production qualification → Recurring shipments → Material financial contribution**

Each step carries different information.

Joint development is not a purchase commitment. Equipment installed in a pilot line does not mean the end product has reached mass adoption. Building a supply system does not guarantee immediate capacity utilization.

DNP's glass-core program illustrates the distinction. The company established a pilot line and planned sample shipments, with the goal of building a full mass-production structure in fiscal 2028. It also said the plan would be adjusted in line with customer and market developments.[2]

Dongwoo Fine-Chem, a Sumitomo Chemical subsidiary, has signed a joint-venture agreement with Samsung Electro-Mechanics to establish a glass-core substrate company. The venture targets a supply system in the second half of fiscal 2027. This is closer to commercialization than an exhibition because the partners are committing capital and planning development, manufacturing, and sales. Public information still does not establish future utilization, customer allocation, or profitability.[6]

The 2027–2028 period is therefore worth watching, but these dates first represent **supplier readiness**, not confirmed dates for broad customer adoption.

## How I Would Decide Whether a Company Truly Benefits

I separate companies into three groups rather than placing all of them inside an “AI beneficiary” basket.

### Companies With Existing Related Revenue

These businesses already sell packaging materials, process chemistry, or manufacturing equipment. The question is whether the new technology increases price, content per package, or market share, not merely whether the company appears on a supply-chain list.

Relevant evidence includes segment revenue, gross margin, customer concentration, and utilization.

### Companies Extending an Existing Capability Into a New Use

These companies already possess glass-processing, panel-manufacturing, ceramic, or chemical-material expertise and are attempting to enter advanced packaging.

Relevant evidence includes qualification results, dedicated capacity, and whether the new business is large enough relative to the existing revenue base.

### Companies Still Validating a New Technology

These companies may have completed a prototype or public demonstration while remaining several stages away from purchasing.

Relevant evidence includes sample customers, qualification schedules, yield, production cost, and a first commercial order.

For every company, I return to four questions:

1. **Technical fit:** Does the product solve a real packaging constraint?
2. **Customer evidence:** Is the milestone a demonstration, qualification, or purchase?
3. **Manufacturing capability:** Can yield, cost, and volume meet customer requirements?
4. **Financial materiality:** Even if shipments begin, are they large enough to change company-wide results?

## Taiwan Glass: Why Association Is Not the Same as Benefit

Taiwan Glass was one of my first questions because I wanted to know whether a traditional materials company could gain new demand from AI.

Its glass-fiber cloth already serves electronic-material applications, but glass-fiber cloth and glass-core substrates are different products. The former may reinforce organic substrates or copper-clad laminates; the latter uses glass as the package substrate's core structure.[11]

Taiwan Glass can therefore be a starting point for broader materials research. It should not be classified as a glass-core beneficiary merely because both products contain the word “glass.” Evidence of a relevant product, customer qualification, or order would be required.

The better method is to identify the exact layer a company supplies, determine whether it is developing a new product, and then ask whether a customer is willing to pay for it.

## What Would Invalidate This Research Direction?

I currently treat glass-core and related materials as a long-term industrial thesis to monitor, not a confirmed short-term earnings theme. Several developments could weaken or invalidate it:

- organic cores, improved resins, or ceramics satisfy most customer requirements;
- through-glass-via, metallization, or cutting yields fail to reach production targets;
- costs decline too slowly and customers delay adoption;
- suppliers complete capacity on schedule while customer adoption moves out;
- initial orders are too small to cover R&D, depreciation, and early yield losses;
- the business remains too small to affect company-wide profit.

Glass gaining share would not automatically mean that incumbent materials suppliers decline. If the AI packaging market continues to expand, an older material could lose share while still increasing absolute shipment volume.

That is why I do not only ask which material will win. Market size, product mix, and actual financial contribution matter at the same time.

## The Three Things I Will Track Next

### Who Has Actually Adopted It?

I am looking for evidence tied to a specific product generation, customer qualification, or purchasing decision, not only a joint-development announcement or trade-show demonstration.

### Has Volume Production Been Delayed?

I will compare the timelines published by DNP, Sumitomo Chemical, and other suppliers to see whether pilot production, sampling, and supply-system milestones remain on schedule.

### Did Shipments Produce Profit?

Even after shipments begin, yield, gross margin, depreciation, and revenue contribution must be examined. A technology opportunity becomes company value only when the business can generate recurring cash flow.

News about new materials will probably continue to multiply. What I want to know is not which company appears in the most AI stories, but which one ultimately turns its technology into a business.
