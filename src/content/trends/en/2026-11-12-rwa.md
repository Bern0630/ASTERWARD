The closer I looked, the less straightforward the answer became. The platform offered direct stocks, tokenized stocks, perpetual contracts, options, and high leverage. Several products could use the same ticker and follow nearly the same price, while giving the holder different rights and risks and sending the order to a different market.

That led to a longer list of questions. Does stock-linked volume on a crypto exchange count as real U.S. equity volume? Does a Nasdaq 100 perpetual become CME volume? If the trade remains inside a crypto venue, can market-maker hedging or cross-market arbitrage still affect the underlying shares and futures?

This article began with that U.S. stock section in my Bybit account and follows the market structure behind each of those products.

## Summary | What are you actually buying on a crypto exchange?

Investors once went to a broker for U.S. shares and to a regulated futures market for equity-index futures. Crypto platforms now offer direct stocks, tokenized stocks, stock-linked perpetuals, options, and contracts for difference (CFDs). Two screens may both say “NVDA” or “Nasdaq 100,” yet give the buyer entirely different rights, risks, and routes to market.

The useful question is not whether these products fit inside one app. It is whether the order actually reaches the U.S. equity or CME futures market, where its volume is recorded, and how a contract that stays inside a crypto venue might still affect the underlying market.

Using Bybit's product lineup as an example, five distinctions matter:

1. **Direct shares, tokenized shares, and stock perpetuals are different assets.**
2. **RWA is a broad term for tokenization. Not every derivative that tracks a share price is a tokenized share.**
3. **Volume in a crypto exchange's internal derivatives market does not automatically become official Nasdaq or CME volume.**
4. **Market-maker hedging, cross-market arbitrage, and token creation or redemption can transmit demand to traditional markets.**
5. **Round-the-clock trading extends price discovery, but it also introduces basis, liquidity, liquidation, and custody risks.**

## 1. RWAs, direct shares, and synthetic derivatives: know what you own

Real-world assets, or RWAs, generally use blockchain tokens to represent off-chain assets or claims connected to them. Shares, bonds, and real-estate income rights can all fall under the label. A token that shares a ticker and price path with a listed company, however, does not necessarily make its holder a shareholder.

In a January 2026 statement, the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission distinguished tokenized securities arranged by an issuer or a third party from structures based on custody or synthetic price exposure. A product's name is not enough. Investors must determine whether the token represents a security interest, a contractual claim against a third party, or merely a price-tracking instrument.[1]

| Product | What the holder receives | Underlying shares? | Typical pricing or trading mechanism | Directly creates U.S. equity volume? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Direct stocks | Economic ownership held through a broker | Yes | Executed in U.S. securities markets | Yes, when executed there |
| Tokenized stocks (xStocks) | A token and contractual rights linked to custodied shares | Issuer claims 1:1 backing | Token secondary market, creation, redemption, and arbitrage | Not when tokens merely change hands |
| Stock perpetuals | Contract profit or loss tied to a stock price | No share required for each contract | Platform order book, index price, mark price, and funding | No, not automatically |
| Options | Rights and obligations defined by strike, expiry, and contract terms | Depends on the option | Premium, volatility, Delta, and other inputs | Trading the option does not automatically create a stock trade |
| CFDs | A contract settling the price difference with a dealer | Usually no | Dealer pricing and hedging | No, not automatically |

### Bybit Stocks: direct shares, not on-chain stock tokens

According to Bybit documentation updated through October 2026, its **Stocks** service offers direct U.S. stocks and ETFs through introducing broker Infra Capital Limited and U.S. broker **Alpaca Securities LLC**. Alpaca holds the shares, executes the orders, and may route them to U.S. exchanges or other venues. Eligible dividends and certain corporate actions appear in the investor's account. Payment and settlement use USDC.[2]

That arrangement differs from trading a contract whose value simply follows NVDA. Even so, receiving the economic interest in a real share does not mean every customer appears by name on the issuer's shareholder register. Custody, voting rights, transfer procedures, and investor protections still depend on the broker and product terms.

### Bybit xStocks: share-backed does not mean every token trade is a stock trade

Bybit's **xStocks** are issued by Backed. The issuer says that corresponding shares support the tokens, that issuance follows the assets held in custody, and that creation, redemption, and arbitrage help anchor the price. Retail trading on Bybit nevertheless takes place mainly in a secondary token market, and ordinary users cannot redeem xStocks directly through Bybit.[3]

If one investor sells an NVDAX token to another, the token changes hands; the U.S. market does not necessarily trade one NVDA share at the same time. The underlying market sees additional activity only when issuance, redemption, inventory management, or hedging produces an actual share trade. A token holder's legal rights can also differ from those of an NVDA shareholder.[1][3]

## 2. 100x leverage: a perpetual contract is not an option

Putting “Stocks,” “Futures,” “Options,” and “100x” near one another in an app does not make them the same product.

A **perpetual contract** is a derivative without the fixed expiry of a conventional futures contract. Traders post margin against a notional position and may periodically pay or receive funding. An **option** is priced from its premium, strike, expiry, Delta, implied volatility, and other variables. Options do not all come with a fixed 100-to-one leverage ratio.

### A simple leverage example

Suppose a trader posts **100 USDT of margin** and uses **100x leverage** to open a **10,000 USDT notional** long position:

- A 0.5% rise in the underlying produces a theoretical unrealized gain of about **50 USDT**.
- A 0.5% decline produces a theoretical unrealized loss of about **50 USDT**.
- A 1% decline produces a theoretical loss equal to the initial **100 USDT** margin.

This linear illustration excludes fees, funding, and slippage. **In practice, liquidation may occur before the loss reaches 100% of the initial margin.** Available leverage also varies by instrument, position tier, jurisdiction, and platform rules. One cannot assume that every stock-linked contract offers 100x.

### What are Bybit PerpOptions?

Introduced in 2026, Bybit's **PerpOptions** are European-style options on the platform's **TradFi perpetual contracts**. They are not direct holdings of U.S. shares or standard U.S.-listed equity options. They settle in USDT at expiry rather than delivering shares. The buyer's maximum loss is generally the premium paid, while a seller may face substantial or even unlimited risk.[4]

An option's approximate effective leverage can be expressed as:

$$
\text{Effective leverage}\approx\frac{\text{Underlying price}\times |\Delta|}{\text{Option price}}
$$

That value changes with the market; it is not a fixed multiple supplied by the platform. A perpetual contract's 100x setting therefore cannot be applied directly to options.

## 3. Is the price tied to Nasdaq? Separate listing venue, execution venue, and reference price

NVIDIA (NVDA) and Apple (AAPL) are listed on Nasdaq, but a Nasdaq listing does not mean every trade occurs on the Nasdaq exchange. U.S.-listed shares can also trade on other exchanges, alternative trading systems, and off-exchange venues. The **consolidated tape** collects reported prices and volume from these regulated market centers.[5]

The same NVDA label can therefore refer to several prices:

- **Cash equity price:** an actual execution in the U.S. stock market.
- **Token price:** the price of an xStock on a platform or blockchain market.
- **Perpetual last price:** the latest trade in a derivatives platform's own order book.
- **Index price:** a reference assembled from external market data.
- **Mark price:** a rule-based price used for risk controls and liquidation.

Bybit says its TradFi perpetual index uses eligible price sources. When traditional markets are closed, stale sources may be excluded and smoothing may be applied. Separate rules and deviation limits govern its mark price.[6]

**Products that reference the same share will not trade at precisely the same price at every moment.**

### When can prices diverge?

1. **U.S. weekends and holidays:** an equally active cash market is unavailable for immediate arbitrage.
2. **Earnings and major news:** venues may absorb the information during different trading hours.
3. **Cascading liquidations:** forced trades can briefly move an internal order book.
4. **Shallow depth:** wide spreads allow a small market order to move the last price.
5. **Constrained token creation or redemption:** backing does not guarantee continuous trading at net asset value.
6. **Different data and settlement rules:** platforms may use different oracles, weights, and risk controls.

If a perpetual trades above the cash share, an arbitrageur might sell the expensive perpetual and buy the cheaper share or a substitute. Costs, trading hours, inventory, stock-borrow limits, and counterparty risk all slow that convergence.

## 4. Does every crypto exchange have its own order book?

Many centralized exchanges use a **central limit order book (CLOB)** for perpetual contracts. Buyers, sellers, and market makers leave orders on that platform, which matches them internally. Two venues can both offer a Nasdaq 100 product without sharing an order book.

Market structures still differ:

- **CEX order book:** trades are matched internally and volume is first recorded in the platform's product.
- **Decentralized exchange:** execution may use an automated market maker pool, an on-chain order book, or a hybrid.
- **CFD dealer:** the dealer or a liquidity provider may quote prices without a public central book.
- **Direct-share brokerage service:** orders may be routed across U.S. venues and reported after execution.

Similar-looking prices can thus conceal very different liquidity. Spread, depth, slippage, fees, and trading hours all matter when comparing execution costs.

## 5. Does Bybit Stocks volume count as real U.S. equity volume?

**Yes—provided the stock order is actually executed in the U.S. securities market and reported under the applicable rules.**

The route can be simplified as follows:

```text
Investor places an order through Bybit Stocks
                    ↓
Partner broker receives, executes, or routes the order
                    ↓
Trade occurs on NYSE, Nasdaq, another exchange, or an eligible venue
                    ↓
Execution is reported under U.S. market rules
                    ↓
Trade enters the relevant equity volume and market data
```

Investor.gov explains that the consolidated tape reports prices and volume in listed shares from different market centers, including exchanges, ATSs, and off-exchange dealers.[5]

Three qualifications matter.

**First, an order is not a trade.** An unfilled order adds no volume.

**Second, passing through a Bybit interface and a partner broker should not turn one share execution into two U.S. stock trades.** Reported securities executions, not the number of service providers, determine volume.

**Third, data vendors do not always show the same universe.** One chart may show a single exchange, while another shows consolidated volume. Licensing, delays, extended hours, and fractional-share reporting can also change the display.

Whenever a screen says “Nasdaq stock volume,” check whether it means the Nasdaq exchange alone or the consolidated U.S. market.

## 6. If a crypto exchange trades a Nasdaq 100 future, does CME record it?

The rule is straightforward: **only trades that actually enter CME's trading and clearing system create volume in the corresponding CME contract.**

| Instrument | What it is | Where volume is primarily recorded |
|---|---|---|
| CME E-mini Nasdaq-100 (NQ) | Exchange-listed equity-index future | CME |
| CME Micro E-mini Nasdaq-100 (MNQ) | Smaller equity-index future | CME |
| CME E-mini S&P 500 (ES) | Exchange-listed equity-index future | CME |
| Crypto-platform Nasdaq-100 perpetual | Derivative based on an index, ETF, or reference price | That crypto platform |
| MT5 US100/NAS100 CFD | Contract settling price differences with a dealer | The CFD dealer and its execution system |

A $1 million notional Nasdaq 100 perpetual trade on a crypto platform does **not** automatically become a $1 million NQ trade at CME. The counterparties, margin, settlement, and venue are different.

If a market maker then buys or sells NQ or MNQ at CME to hedge that crypto exposure, the new futures trade does count toward CME volume.

## 7. How can a contract outside CME still affect real share prices?

The key question is who must hedge in the underlying market.

### Channel A: market-maker Delta hedging

Suppose traders collectively buy Nasdaq 100 perpetuals on a crypto exchange, leaving liquidity providers with more short exposure. To control risk, a market maker may buy an offsetting position on another platform, in CME NQ, in an ETF, or in a basket of shares.

```text
Buying pressure in a crypto index perpetual
                    ↓
Liquidity provider takes the other side
                    ↓
Market maker measures net Delta and risk limits
                    ↓
It may buy CME NQ, an ETF, or constituent shares as a hedge
                    ↓
Only an executed hedge enters that traditional market's volume
```

This is one **possible route**, not an automatic one-for-one response. A market maker may already have an offsetting position, hedge elsewhere, or temporarily retain the exposure.

### Channel B: cash–futures arbitrage

When CME equity-index futures trade above theoretical fair value, an arbitrageur may sell the expensive future and buy a share basket or related ETF. The direction may reverse when futures are cheap.

Fair value is not always equal to the cash index. Interest rates, expected dividends, and time to expiry also matter. CME describes how spot value, carrying cost, and dividends enter this calculation.[7]

Index-futures prices can therefore change the incentive to trade cash equities, but futures volume and share volume do not map one for one.

### Channel C: creation and redemption of tokenized shares

If sustained demand for a share-backed token causes new tokens to be issued, the issuer or an authorized participant may need more underlying inventory. Redemption and a reduction in inventory may create selling demand.

But a token changing hands 100 times in the secondary market does not mean its underlying share traded 100 times.

### Channel D: option Gamma hedging

An option market maker's Delta changes with the underlying price. Keeping risk within limits may require repeated adjustments in shares, ETFs, or futures. This can amplify or dampen moves in the cash market.

The direction depends on the market maker's **net Gamma**, position distribution, liquidity, and hedge choice. “More option volume means more stock volatility” is not a reliable rule.

## 8. Can volumes simply be added? Volume, open interest, and capital flow are different

Consider a hypothetical day:

| Market | Hypothetical daily notional volume |
|---|---:|
| CME NQ futures | $200 billion |
| Crypto exchange A index perpetual | $10 billion |
| Crypto exchange B index perpetual | $5 billion |
| Related contracts on CFD platforms | $3 billion |

These figures do not represent $218 billion of independent inflows, nor do they imply that all positions are new longs. A contract can change hands repeatedly. Cross-market hedging can also make the same underlying risk appear in several venues' notional volume.

Three measures must be kept separate:

- **Volume:** completed trading during a period, not one-way buying.
- **Open interest (OI):** futures or option contracts that remain open; interpretation requires information about opening, closing, and positioning.
- **Net exposure:** directional risk remaining after offsetting positions and hedges.

Every matched trade has a buyer and a seller. A surge in volume alone does not show that capital is universally “flowing into longs.”

Research into incremental risk from crypto derivatives should consider volume, OI, funding, cross-market basis, order-book depth, and simultaneous activity in traditional markets.

## 9. Will 24/7 U.S. equity exposure change price discovery?

When cash equities are closed but tokens or perpetuals still trade, crypto venues may react first to earnings, geopolitics, or policy news. Prices can adjust again through arbitrage when deeper traditional markets reopen.

One possible sequence is:

```text
Material news arrives while U.S. equities are closed
                    ↓
Stock-linked crypto derivatives move first
                    ↓
CME futures reopen and incorporate the information
                    ↓
Arbitrage, market-maker hedging, and basis trading follow
                    ↓
Shares and ETFs reprice when the cash market opens
```

**The sequence will not run in the same direction every time.** A thin crypto market with wide spreads or weak reference data may be corrected after CME or cash trading resumes. Whether it is a useful leading indicator requires long-run evidence, not a few weekend examples.

At least three questions deserve testing:

1. Do crypto equity-index derivatives quoted while U.S. shares are closed predict returns after CME reopens?
2. When crypto OI, basis, and liquidations rise, do CME volume, volatility, and spreads change at the same time?
3. Are tokenized-share creations and redemptions large enough to produce a measurable cash-price effect in less-liquid stocks?

These are **research hypotheses**, not established universal causal relationships.

## 10. What should an intraday trader actually monitor?

| Research goal | Primary data | Additional context |
|---|---|---|
| Assess U.S. cash-equity activity | Consolidated U.S. equity volume, quotes, and depth | Individual venues and extended-hours differences |
| Monitor Nasdaq 100 futures | CME NQ/MNQ volume and depth | OI, cash–futures basis, major economic releases |
| Monitor S&P 500 futures | CME ES/MES volume and depth | Term structure and cash ETFs |
| Monitor Dow futures | CME YM/MYM | Constituent performance and index weights |
| Assess crypto leverage risk | Contract OI, funding, and liquidation data by platform | Leverage limits and margin tiers |
| Evaluate cross-market spreads | Cash, ETF, CME, and crypto quotes | Timestamps, costs, and market-making depth |

A trader should not substitute crypto index-perpetual volume for CME NQ or ES volume. Nor is a platform's stock or index “24h change” necessarily comparable with the return over a conventional U.S. trading day.

For leveraged products, verify the settlement currency, mark price, liquidation mechanism, funding, price source while the cash market is closed, and whether the product is available in your jurisdiction.

## Conclusion | Prices cross borders; volume stays with its venue

Crypto exchanges are making traditional finance and digital-asset markets more directly connected. A shared ticker does not make two products the same asset, however, and tracking the same index does not send their volume to the same exchange.

A direct share trade executed and reported in the U.S. securities market becomes part of the relevant equity data. CME index futures have their own trading and clearing statistics. Perpetuals and options matched inside a crypto platform remain volume in that platform's market; **they do not automatically count toward official Nasdaq or CME volume.**

Prices have less tidy borders. Delta and Gamma hedging, cash–futures arbitrage, and the creation or redemption of share-backed tokens can turn demand in one venue into real orders in another.

The questions worth following are therefore: **How much genuine exposure is accumulating in these new markets? Who holds the risk? How much of it returns to traditional equity and futures markets through hedging and arbitrage?** Those answers reveal more about changing price discovery than a simple count of stock tickers inside a crypto app.
