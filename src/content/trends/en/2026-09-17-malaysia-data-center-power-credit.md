> Capacity methodology: figures retain their source definitions; announced, contracted, under-construction, energized and utilized capacity are not treated as interchangeable.

## What Is Changing

Malaysia's data-center theme is shifting from the amount of investment attracted to the capacity that can secure power, energize on schedule and produce debt-serviceable cash flow. MIDA reported RM385.7 billion of data-center-related investment from 2021 through the first half of 2026 and projected electricity demand above 5,000 MW by 2035. More importantly, MIDA cited an industry estimate of approximately 4.6 GW of capacity that is planned or under construction. That is not operational capacity.

The available capacity map is:

| Stage | Confirmed information | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Announced, planned or under construction | Approximately 4.6 GW, with planned and under-construction capacity combined in the source | It is not necessarily energized or financing-secured |
| Electricity supply agreements signed | 49 data centers with 7.1 GW of aggregate maximum demand, phased over five to ten years | It is not simultaneous commissioning or full utilization |
| Actual load | Approximately 710 MW in September 2025 | It is not the same as nationwide operational IT load or pre-leased capacity |
| 2035 demand projection | More than 5,000 MW; the regulator estimates data centers could reach 31% of Peninsular Malaysia demand | It is a forecast, not approved or financing-secured capacity |

The distance between 7.1 GW of signed maximum demand and 710 MW of actual load does not mean demand is fictitious. It means delivery is multi-year and cash flow must wait for generation, transmission, substations, cooling, network access, tenant move-in and equipment utilization.

## Why Now

First, data centers reached a record 9.3% of Malaysian electricity use in the second week of August 2026, above this year's average of about 7%, as hot weather increased cooling demand. Second, Malaysia's energy regulator identified a roughly 9 GW gas-generation capacity gap to be filled by 2032. Data-center growth is therefore moving from corporate investment into national power planning.

Third, after the Fed hike, US long-term yields are near 5% and Malaysia's ten-year government yield is approximately 4.22%. Even with Bank Negara Malaysia holding the OPR at 2.75%, data centers and grids are priced off global capital costs, project-finance spreads and currency risk. Fourth, MIDA has explicitly shifted policy emphasis from additional capacity toward local supply chains, AI capability, high-value employment, power efficiency and water efficiency. Market valuation criteria will move in the same direction.

## End Demand

End demand comes from cloud services, AI training and inference, enterprise digitalization, data sovereignty, disaster recovery and regional spillover from Singapore's capacity constraints. Investable demand must answer four questions: whether compute is actually used, whether customers will pay over a long period, whether rack density fits the power and cooling design, and whether network latency meets the workload requirement.

Training workloads create large, concentrated demand but are exposed to chip generations and changes in model efficiency. Inference can be more distributed and persistent, but utilization and pricing competition are harder to forecast. GPU and ASIC shipments, cloud utilization, compute revenue per unit of electricity, tenant prepayments and minimum-purchase commitments reveal more about demand quality than announced megawatts alone.

## Business Model

Data centers convert land, power, substations, cooling, water, fiber, server space and debt into long-term contracted cash flow. Models include hyperscaler self-builds, third-party colocation, wholesale leasing, cloud services and GPU as a service. Moving closer to compute services can raise growth but also adds equipment-residual-value, technology-obsolescence and utilization risk. Moving closer to real-estate leasing can stabilize revenue, but power cost, tenant concentration and refinancing become more important.

The core metrics are not floor area. They are energized IT load, pre-leasing, actual utilization, weighted average lease expiry, single-tenant concentration, net operating income, cap rates, loan-to-value, debt-service coverage, interest coverage, weighted average cost of capital and the debt maturity schedule.

## Who Pays

Ultimate payers include hyperscalers such as AWS, Microsoft and Google, enterprises, AI companies, governments and telecommunications customers. Credit quality depends on the contracting entity, payment priority and termination rights, not merely whether a prominent brand appears in a project announcement.

Long-term minimum purchases, prepayments, take-or-pay provisions, parent guarantees and residual-value guarantees can improve financeability, but they can also enable double counting. If the same compute demand is recorded as committed by a cloud provider, reseller, GPU financing vehicle and data-center owner, the market can manufacture ghost demand.

## Who Borrows

Borrowers can be data-center developers, REITs, infrastructure funds, utilities, telecommunications companies, project companies or special-purpose vehicles holding GPUs. Banks provide construction loans and revolving facilities, bond markets provide term funding, and private credit may finance construction bridges or riskier equipment.

The ultimate credit bearer may differ from the visible asset owner. Non-recourse special-purpose debt leaves creditors exposed to utilization and residual value. Parent guarantees return the risk to the corporate balance sheet. If utilities build the grid before data centers arrive, some risk may migrate to ratepayers or the government.

## Capital Structure

A sound capital structure combines secured land and power access, meaningful pre-leasing before construction, long-dated fixed-rate funding, conservative loan-to-value, interest-coverage headroom, phased capex, and separate financing for equipment and real estate. The building may last 15 to 25 years, but GPUs, network and cooling equipment have shorter economic lives. Long-lived real-estate valuations should not be used to finance short-lived equipment without additional protection.

A weak structure uses short-dated floating-rate debt at a high valuation and assumes rent growth, utilization and rate cuts will arrive together. Supplier finance, circular prepayments, cross-holdings and special-purpose vehicles can hide leverage. Those obligations should be recombined when assessing the economic capital structure.

## Credit Structure

The credit chain is: delayed power access leads to delayed energization; tenant rent commencement is deferred; net operating income undershoots; interest and debt-service coverage deteriorate; lower asset values raise loan-to-value; and the project must add collateral, refinance or default.

Equity and credit can diverge. Equity may reward utilities, contractors, power equipment, industrial land and telecommunications companies for new orders. Credit focuses on who funds substations, gas generation, transmission and reserve capacity before demand materializes, and whether tenants carry minimum-payment obligations. Deteriorating loan terms, bond spreads or CDS would be an early warning even if order-driven equities continue to rise.

There is no current evidence that Malaysian data centers have created a systemic credit event. Near-term risk remains concentrated at the project, tenant and grid-delivery level. If the 7.1 GW of signed maximum demand moves rapidly into construction without matching generation and transmission, risk can spread into bank assets, public spending and electricity-pricing policy.

## Bull Case

The bull case requires timely grid construction and delivery of 9 GW of additional gas capacity, with renewable and corporate procurement mechanisms supporting low-carbon demand. Anchor tenants sign long leases and minimum-payment commitments, while actual load rises steadily from 710 MW. If the OPR remains 2.75%, the ringgit is stable and projects secure long-dated fixed-rate financing, construction orders can become durable cash flow.

The likely beneficiary order is transmission and transformers, generation and gas supply, construction, fiber and telecommunications, industrial property with land and power, and only then highly valued developers without energized capacity. The bull case is complete only when equity gains coincide with stable funding spreads, higher pre-leasing and rising actual load.

## Bear Case

The bear case is that hot weather, gas supply and transmission bottlenecks delay power connections while US and Malaysian long yields remain elevated. A weaker ringgit raises the cost of imported equipment and dollar debt. If greater chip efficiency or lower hyperscaler capex reduces demand, announced capacity will face lower pre-leasing and rent pressure.

The worst path is not an overnight disappearance of demand. It is persistent utilization below underwriting, repeated refinancing and declining equipment residual values, ultimately creating stranded assets. If utilities have already funded the grid, developers have borrowed and tenants delay move-in, losses are redistributed among equity holders, banks, bond investors and ratepayers.

## Weakest Link

The weakest projects are announced but lack firm power access, complete financing and high-quality pre-leasing. The most exposed combine short-dated floating-rate debt, tenant concentration, rapid equipment obsolescence and no parent guarantee inside a special-purpose vehicle.

The second weak link is front-loaded generation and transmission capex. The 7.1 GW of maximum demand under 49 supply agreements will be phased over five to ten years, but grid investment often must occur earlier. If demand arrives more slowly than the grid, utilization and the regulatory cost-recovery mechanism become utility credit risks.

## Leading Indicators

1. Actual load rising sustainably above 710 MW, not just higher maximum demand in supply agreements.
2. Separate disclosure of power-secured, energized, pre-leased and actually utilized megawatts.
3. Completion of substations, transmission, gas generation and reserve power.
4. Power usage effectiveness, water usage effectiveness, rack density and cooling cost.
5. Pre-leasing, weighted average lease expiry, tenant concentration, take-or-pay provisions and parent guarantees.
6. Loan-to-value, debt-service coverage, interest coverage, maturity walls and refinancing spreads.
7. Ten-year MGS yields, the ringgit, bank lending standards, corporate spreads and private-credit terms.
8. Hyperscaler capex, cloud utilization, GPU and ASIC shipments, and measured adoption of training and inference workloads.

## What the Market May Be Missing

The market may be treating growth in power demand as equivalent to growth in high-return data-center assets. The 710 MW of actual load in September 2025, 7.1 GW of signed maximum demand and the projection above 5,000 MW for 2035 refer to different stages. They can all be accurate while implying very different near-term revenue, cash flow and debt capacity.

A second blind spot is that secured power does not equal secured financing. Even with an electricity supply agreement, a project must establish the generation source, transmission node, connection date, power-cost pass-through, tenant rent commencement and construction-overrun protection. The most valuable disclosure is not another investment headline. It is how many megawatts are power-secured, financing-secured, pre-leased, energized and actually used at each stage.

## Next Confirmation

The next confirmations are whether TNB and the regulator update actual load, phasing under the 49 supply agreements and generation additions; whether MIDA and developers split the 4.6 GW of planned or under-construction capacity into power-secured, financing-secured, under-construction, energized and pre-leased stages; and whether listed companies disclose tenants, lease duration, capex, debt and interest coverage.

Rising actual load, pre-leasing and cash flow alongside stable financing spreads would validate the growth thesis. More announced capacity alongside weaker utilization, pre-leasing and credit terms would be an early signal of overbuilding and stranded assets.
