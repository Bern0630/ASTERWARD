## What Is Changing

Malaysia's data-center theme is shifting from new capacity and foreign investment toward power, financing and credit risk. The market has focused on announced capacity, anchor tenants, land and utility beneficiaries. The more important questions now are whether power is secured, who funds the capex, whether tenants are creditworthy, and whether contracted cash flow can support debt.

## Why Now

There are three reasons. First, global rates are rising again, lifting the weighted average cost of capital for heavy infrastructure assets. Second, AI inference and training are increasing rack density, making power and cooling not just operating costs but core delivery constraints. Third, Malaysia has become a major Southeast Asian data-center destination. The faster the cycle grows, the more investors must distinguish operational capacity from announced capacity.

## End Demand

End demand comes from cloud services, AI inference, enterprise digitalization, data sovereignty and regional redundancy. The real demand is not for more buildings. It is for compute capacity with power, network access, cooling and regulatory suitability. Demand quality is stronger when end customers sign long-term contracts and absorb part of the cost. It is weaker when it depends on short-term subleasing or unproven AI demand.

## Business Model

The surface business model is leasing data-center capacity. The actual model is converting land, power, cooling, network access and debt into long-term infrastructure cash flow. The key metrics are pre-leasing, weighted average lease expiry, tenant credit, net operating income, cap rates, loan-to-value, debt-service coverage and interest coverage.

## Who Pays

Cloud service providers, AI companies, enterprise customers and some sovereign or government-linked demand ultimately pay. The key issue is not the contract label, but the customer's ability and willingness to pay. If the tenant still relies on external financing to subsidize compute demand, the data-center owner is effectively taking tenant business-model risk.

## Who Borrows

Borrowers may include data-center developers, REITs, infrastructure funds, utilities or project companies. If projects rely on high leverage, short-dated debt or floating-rate funding, higher rates directly compress equity returns and increase refinancing risk.

## Capital Structure

A healthier structure has more long-dated fixed-rate funding, clear pre-leasing, secured power access, conservative loan-to-value and adequate interest coverage. A weaker structure announces capacity first, then looks for tenants and financing, while assuming future rates will decline.

## Credit Structure

Credit risk transmits through demand, revenue, cash flow, debt, asset value, loan-to-value and default risk. If tenants delay move-in, utilization undershoots, power costs rise or refinancing rates exceed underwriting assumptions, creditor protection thins quickly.

## Bull Case

The bull case is that Malaysia maintains stable rates and currency conditions, data-center projects secure power, anchor tenants sign long leases, pre-leasing improves, and banks and bond investors provide long-tenor funding. In that scenario, power equipment, utilities, construction, selected REITs and E&E exports can benefit.

## Bear Case

The bear case is that US rates stay high, the ringgit weakens, funding costs rise, power connections are delayed, and announced capacity fails to become energized and utilized capacity. If tenant demand disappoints or AI compute prices fall, data-center asset values fall and creditors demand wider spreads.

## Weakest Link

The weakest link is not an operating asset with strong tenants. It is a project without secured power, completed financing or adequate pre-leasing, but with high leverage and aggressive valuation. These projects are most exposed to the mistake of treating announced capacity as cash-flowing capacity.

## Leading Indicators

The leading indicators are power-connection approvals, power purchase agreements, substation and transmission progress, pre-leasing, tenant lists, contract duration, project-finance rates, loan-to-value, debt-service coverage, Malaysian government bond yields, the ringgit, bank lending standards and utility capex.

## What the Market May Be Missing

The market may be underestimating the gap between growth in power demand and growth in investable cash flow. Demand can exist without a project securing power and financing at a reasonable cost. Demand also does not guarantee that tenants will lease capacity long term at prices high enough to justify the asset value.

## Next Confirmation

The next confirmation points are whether Malaysia accelerates grid investment, whether data-center projects disclose secured power and financing, whether anchor tenants sign long-term contracts, and whether related companies can maintain interest coverage at higher rates.
