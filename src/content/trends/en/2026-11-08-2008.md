Yesterday, I watched an older film called *Too Big to Fail*. It focuses on the most frantic weeks of the 2008 financial crisis: Lehman Brothers cannot find a buyer, AIG is close to collapse, and U.S. officials and Wall Street executives move from meeting to meeting, deciding which institutions to rescue, how to do it, and who will bear the cost.

What stayed with me after the film were the questions it did not have time to answer in full. Did the crisis really begin on the day Lehman failed? Why did the U.S. government support Bear Stearns and AIG but not Lehman? What actually happened with Buffett, Korea Development Bank, Barclays, and the capital injections into nine major banks? And when reports cite enormous rescue figures, are they referring to a credit limit, an equity investment, the face value of an asset, or a loss ultimately borne by the government?

This article began with those questions. It follows the chain from the deterioration of the mortgage market in 2007, through the breakdown of funding in 2008, to the government's gradual exit from the AIG rescue in 2013. The film is the point of entry. The account that follows relies mainly on official investigations, regulatory announcements, and disclosures by the institutions involved, restoring the years of financial history compressed into a few weeks on screen.

## 01 | Start with the conclusion: Lehman did not create the crisis in a day

The global financial crisis grew out of the U.S. housing bubble, loose lending standards, securitization, leveraged funding, failed risk management, and a financial system whose institutions were tightly connected. Home prices began weakening around 2006. Subprime defaults rose, mortgage-backed securities and collateralized debt obligations lost value, and financial firms suffered damage to both collateral and capital. Funds had already failed and markets had already become unstable in 2007. The U.S. recession began in December of that year.

Lehman Brothers' bankruptcy on September 15, 2008 was therefore not the start of the crisis. It sharply accelerated a crisis already under way.

**The main chain of transmission:**

```text
Expectations of rising home prices and weaker lending standards
                         │
                         ▼
Subprime securitization (RMBS and CDOs) plus CDS protection
                         │
                         ▼
Falling home prices, rising defaults, and lower collateral values
                         │
                         ▼
Write-downs at banks and investment banks; capital is impaired
                         │
                         ▼
Short-term funding retreats; margin and collateral calls rise
                         │
                         ▼
Bear Stearns crisis → Lehman bankruptcy → emergency AIG rescue
                         │
                         ▼
Stress in money-market funds and commercial paper; banks lose trust
                         │
                         ▼
Public capital, central-bank liquidity, acquisitions, restructuring
                         │
                         ▼
Damage to business credit and consumption; global recession and reform
```

## 02 | A complete timeline, 2007–2013

### 2007 | The crisis surfaces

**2006–2007 | The U.S. housing market weakens**  
Mortgage delinquencies rise and valuations of subprime-linked securities come under scrutiny.

**June–July 2007 | Two Bear Stearns hedge funds collapse**  
Highly leveraged strategies concentrated in subprime securities fail.

**August 9, 2007 | BNP Paribas freezes trading in three funds**  
The French bank cannot reliably value some holdings, showing that U.S. mortgage risk has crossed borders.

**December 2007 | The U.S. recession begins**  
The NBER later dates the start of the recession to nine months before Lehman's bankruptcy.

### First half of 2008 | The first large institutions seek help

**March 13–16 | Bear Stearns runs short of liquidity; JPMorgan announces an acquisition**  
The Fed supports the resolution of selected assets to avoid a disorderly failure.

**March 28 | Warren Buffett and Lehman CEO Richard Fuld discuss an investment**  
They do not agree on the preferred-share yield or the warrant and conversion terms. Buffett also has serious concerns of his own.

**April 1 | Lehman raises roughly $4 billion in convertible preferred shares**  
The capital helps, but does not cure its asset and funding problems.

**June | Lehman reports a quarterly loss and raises more capital**  
Commercial real-estate and mortgage exposures continue to weigh on the firm.

**July 11 | Regulators seize IndyMac**  
The damage is no longer confined to investment banks; a traditional deposit-taking bank also fails.

### September 2008 | The turning point

**September 7 | Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac enter conservatorship**  
The federal government intervenes in the two housing-finance giants.

**September 9 | News that Korea Development Bank will not proceed with a Lehman investment hits the market**  
Lehman loses a major prospective source of capital and its shares plunge.

**September 12–14 | The New York Fed, Treasury, SEC, and financial executives negotiate**  
They explore a Barclays or Bank of America acquisition and ways for private firms to share the risk of Lehman's troubled assets.

**September 14 | Talks for a full Barclays acquisition fail**  
The unresolved issues include guarantees before closing, the need for U.K. shareholder approval, and who would bear the risk.

**September 15 | Lehman Brothers Holdings Inc. files for Chapter 11**  
The parent company files; not every Lehman legal entity enters the same process at the same time.

**September 15 | Bank of America announces its acquisition of Merrill Lynch**  
The transaction closes on January 1, 2009.

**September 15 | Major rating agencies downgrade AIG**  
Collateral calls on its credit-default swaps and its cash shortfall rise sharply.

**September 16 | The Fed announces an AIG credit facility of up to $85 billion**  
The facility is designed to prevent an immediate disorderly failure. The Reserve Primary Fund breaks the buck the same day.

**September 19 | The United States announces a temporary guarantee for money-market funds**  
The measure seeks to contain redemptions and prevent a breakdown in short-term funding.

**September 21 | Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley become bank holding companies**  
The two firms do not merge with each other or with another large bank.

**September 22–23 | MUFG and Morgan Stanley announce their intentions; Buffett invests in Goldman**  
Berkshire Hathaway announces its $5 billion Goldman transaction on September 23.

**September 25 | Washington Mutual Bank is seized**  
JPMorgan acquires its banking operations.

**September 29 | MUFG and Morgan Stanley announce a definitive $9 billion agreement**  
The House rejects the first TARP bill on the same day.

### October–December 2008 | The rescue expands

**October 3 | Congress passes and the president signs the $700 billion TARP**  
The figure is the original authorization ceiling, not a same-day cash payment.

**October 8 | The Fed announces an additional AIG securities-lending facility**  
The facility can provide up to about $37.8 billion.

**October 13 | MUFG completes its $9 billion investment in Morgan Stanley**  
Treasury also meets with the heads of nine large financial institutions about public capital.

**October 14 | Treasury announces the Capital Purchase Program**  
The initial allocation is up to $250 billion; $125 billion is approved for the first nine institutions.

**October 28 | The first eight banks receive $115 billion in CPP capital**  
Merrill's planned $10 billion is delayed until its merger closes.

**November 10 | The AIG rescue is restructured**  
The plan adds $40 billion of TARP preferred shares and Maiden Lane II and III, shifting part of the response from repeated lending toward capital and the resolution of troubled assets and contracts.

**November–December | Maiden Lane III buys CDOs and terminates CDS contracts in stages**  
Maiden Lane II buys RMBS in December, helping wind down AIG's related securities-lending program.

**December | The Fed lowers its target range to 0–0.25%**  
Monetary policy moves further into unconventional territory.

### 2009–2013 | Restructuring, recession, and reform

**January 1, 2009 | Bank of America completes the Merrill Lynch acquisition**  
The closing comes with further concerns about capital and troubled assets.

**May 7, 2009 | The United States releases stress-test results for 19 large banks**  
The Supervisory Capital Assessment Program makes potential capital gaps more visible.

**From June 2009 | Some large banks repay TARP**  
Financial conditions begin to stabilize, while unemployment and business activity remain weak.

**July 2010 | Dodd–Frank becomes law**  
The law addresses systemic importance, derivatives, non-bank financial firms, and crisis resolution.

**December 2012 | Treasury sells its remaining AIG common shares**  
The government continues to exit its rescue investment.

**March 2013 | The disposition of the remaining AIG warrants is completed**  
The public rescue enters its final financial stage, even as structural changes to the system continue.

## 03 | The instruments and mechanics: how U.S. mortgages damaged global banks

A **subprime mortgage** is a home loan to a borrower with weaker credit. **RMBS and MBS** turn mortgage cash flows into securities. A **CDO** repackages and tranches debt exposures. A **CDS** is a derivative contract that provides protection against a defined credit risk. These are not interchangeable: an MBS is an asset-backed security, a CDO is a structured pool of exposure, and a CDS is contractual credit protection.

Securitization alone did not cause the crisis. The breakdown also involved poor underwriting, optimistic assumptions about asset prices, model-dependent ratings, hard-to-value assets, leverage, and maturity mismatch. A bank that funds long-dated, illiquid assets with short-term borrowing can fail when creditors refuse to roll that funding, even before its accounts show it to be insolvent.

Consider a simplified balance sheet with $100 billion in assets, $97 billion in liabilities, and $3 billion in equity. Assets are roughly 33.3 times equity. A 3% decline in asset value could nearly erase that equity, all else equal. If counterparties demand more collateral at the same time, the firm may have to sell assets into a falling market and recognize still more losses.

## 04 | Bear Stearns: why the Fed could rescue the first firm

By March 2008, Bear Stearns could no longer obtain enough liquidity in ordinary markets. JPMorgan was willing to buy it. Through Maiden Lane, the New York Fed supported roughly $30 billion of risky assets: the Fed lent about $29 billion and JPMorgan took the first $1 billion of losses. The initial acquisition price was about $2 per share and was later raised to $10.

This was not a $29 billion gift from the Fed to JPMorgan. It was a special-purpose asset-financing arrangement meant to prevent Bear's failure from spreading through repo, foreign-exchange, derivatives, and clearing markets. [Federal Reserve: Bear Stearns, JPMorgan, and Maiden Lane](https://www.federalreserve.gov/regreform/reform-bearstearns.htm)

The rescue nevertheless raised an uncomfortable question: would every large investment bank receive public support? That is the moral-hazard problem. It does not prove that officials later allowed Lehman to fail simply to punish the market.

## 05 | Lehman's missed opportunities: Buffett, KDB, and capital raising

### Buffett did not simply offer to buy common shares at $40

The film turns Richard Fuld's rejection of Buffett's return demands into a dramatic hinge. The real March 2008 discussion involved preferred shares yielding around 9% and stock rights around $40 per share. Buffett had not made an unconditional offer to buy all of Lehman's common stock at $40. The shares were then trading in the high $30s.

Fuld worried about the dividend cost and dilution. Lehman instead raised roughly $4 billion on April 1 through convertible preferred shares paying 7.25%. That financing appeared cheaper, but a lower coupon did not guarantee restored confidence. Interviews in the bankruptcy examiner's report also show that Buffett had concerns about Lehman's asset quality, management, and disclosures after reviewing its accounts. Fuld's approval alone would not necessarily have produced a $5 billion Buffett investment, and the film's dialogue is not an official recording.

### The Korea Development Bank talks collapse

KDB considered a capital injection, a major stake, and potentially a broader transaction. The parties did not agree on valuation, asset risk, or terms; the Korean side also faced volatile markets and constraints of its own. News on September 9 that talks had ended sent Lehman's stock sharply lower. The failure damaged both funding prospects and confidence. It cannot be reduced to one person's refusal.

### Repo 105 and the presentation of leverage

Lehman used transactions including Repo 105 to reduce reported assets and liabilities around reporting dates. The practice made its underlying leverage harder for outsiders to judge. The bankruptcy examiner raised serious questions about the accounting and disclosure. The film gives this governance issue little attention.

## 06 | The final 72 hours: who took part in the Lehman talks?

From September 12 to 14, Treasury Secretary **Henry “Hank” Paulson**, New York Fed President **Timothy “Tim” Geithner**, Fed Chair **Ben Bernanke**, SEC Chair **Christopher Cox**, and senior Wall Street executives held intensive negotiations.

Their positions, inferred from actions and public records rather than film dialogue, included:

- **Richard Fuld / Lehman:** sought capital or a buyer while resisting a fire-sale valuation and severe dilution.
- **Jamie Dimon / JPMorgan:** focused on the clearing bank's counterparty exposure, collateral, and protection of JPMorgan's funds.
- **Lloyd Blankfein / Goldman Sachs:** participated in the talks while protecting Goldman's capital and funding.
- **John Mack / Morgan Stanley:** feared that markets would turn on the remaining independent investment banks.
- **Vikram Pandit / Citigroup:** faced significant asset and capital pressure inside his own group.
- **Ken Lewis / Bank of America:** compared Lehman with Merrill and the troubled assets each deal might bring.
- **John Thain / Merrill Lynch:** sought a survival plan for Merrill and ultimately reached an agreement with BofA.
- **John Varley and Bob Diamond / Barclays:** saw value in the investment-banking business but would not accept the entire group's obligations without limits.

This is not a complete attendance list for a single meeting. The film compresses several meetings, calls, and stages of negotiation.

## 07 | Barclays had scale. Why could it not buy all of Lehman?

At the end of 2007, Barclays had roughly **£1.23 trillion in assets** and **£32.5 billion in shareholder equity**. It owned a deposit bank, the fixed-income and foreign-exchange operations of Barclays Capital, Barclaycard, and what was then Barclays Global Investors and iShares. It had both scale and strategic interest in Lehman's U.S. equities, investment-banking franchise, and client network.

Total assets, however, are neither available cash nor loss-absorbing equity. The U.K. **Financial Services Authority** supervised the bank. The impasse concerned whether Barclays could guarantee large and uncertain obligations before closing, how required shareholder approval could be obtained, and whether a government would provide interim protection. The parties never reached an executable arrangement for the whole group.

The film makes the episode look like an agreed rescue stopped by one FSA phone call. In reality, the proposal still had unresolved legal, liquidity, and guarantee problems. After Lehman's bankruptcy, Barclays bought **parts of the North American investment-banking and brokerage operations** through a bankruptcy-court-approved sale completed on September 22. Paying about $1.75 billion for those selected operations was not the same as buying all of Lehman.

## 08 | Why did the SEC press Lehman to file?

By the evening of September 14, acquisition talks had failed and Lehman's liquidity was disappearing. Treasury, the New York Fed, and the SEC strongly pressed the company to enter Chapter 11 before global markets opened. The SEC did not issue an administrative order declaring the holding company bankrupt. Lehman's board and the company made the formal filing. The pressure shown in the film has a basis in the record, but the dialogue is not a verbatim transcript.

The September 15 debtor was **Lehman Brothers Holdings Inc.**, the parent. Its U.S. broker-dealer, LBI, did not enter the identical proceeding at the same moment. It continued some clearing and operations with facilities including the Fed's PDCF before selected operations were sold to Barclays.

Why did the Fed support Bear Stearns but not Lehman? Officials said Lehman lacked both a viable acquisition and sufficient collateral for the lending it needed, while Treasury did not yet have TARP-like authority to inject large amounts of equity. Critics argue that officials underestimated the consequences or still had alternatives. The issue remains contested; “moral hazard” and “they chose to let it die” are not complete explanations.

## 09 | Why did AIG need a rescue immediately after Lehman?

AIG's risks did not suddenly appear after Lehman's failure. Falling mortgage prices had already damaged two businesses.

**AIG Financial Products' CDS portfolio:** AIG had sold protection on CDOs and other credit assets held by banks around the world. Falling CDO values and rating downgrades triggered requirements to post more cash collateral. Those collateral calls were not the same as final realized credit losses.

**Securities lending at insurance subsidiaries:** The subsidiaries lent securities, received cash collateral, and invested some of that cash in residential mortgage securities. When borrowers wanted their cash back, the RMBS had fallen in value and become hard to sell, creating a second liquidity shortfall.

Lehman's September 15 bankruptcy intensified the panic. AIG was downgraded that day, increasing its collateral calls just as private fundraising became harder. AIG and Lehman shared roots in the mortgage crisis, but Lehman's collapse did not single-handedly create AIG's problems.

### Why rescue AIG but not Lehman?

1. **Systemic exposure:** AIG was connected to U.S. and European banks, policyholders, retirement products, money markets, and securities lending. An uncontrolled collapse threatened a broad chain reaction. Lehman was also systemically important; the distinction was not that it had no links to other banks.
2. **Legal authority and collateral then available:** Fed officials concluded that AIG had assets capable of securing emergency lending under Section 13(3), while Lehman lacked what they considered a workable transaction and collateral package. That judgment remains disputed.
3. **The policy environment changed after September 15:** Once markets had absorbed the shock of Lehman's failure, officials were less willing to risk a second disorderly collapse.

The **$85 billion** announced on September 16 was a maximum emergency credit line, secured by assets, carrying expensive terms, and accompanied by an arrangement giving the government an interest of about 79.9%. It was not a grant. [New York Fed: the AIG rescue](https://www.newyorkfed.org/aboutthefed/aig)

## 10 | Why were France, Germany, and other countries pulled in?

AIG Financial Products had sold CDS protection to multinational banks and maintained securities-lending and other financial relationships across borders. Important counterparties included **Société Générale, Calyon (later Crédit Agricole CIB), Goldman Sachs, Deutsche Bank, UBS, and Barclays**. Some banks used CDS to manage credit exposure or regulatory capital. If AIG defaulted, they could lose hedges, need more capital, or suffer credit losses.

Publicly compiled payments associated with Société Générale and AIG totaled about **$16.5 billion**, including collateral received before the rescue, additional collateral afterward, and Maiden Lane III payments. This was neither a $16.5 billion gift from the U.S. government nor bank profit. The rescue did indirectly support performance of international contracts, and the decision to settle with counterparties near par drew criticism.

France also faced the global shortage of dollar funding and the broader credit shock, including the Dexia crisis and domestic stability measures. Not every problem at a French bank can be attributed to AIG.

## 11 | How the AIG rescue worked: four layers of financing and resolution

**Layer one: September 16, 2008 — a credit line of up to $85 billion.** It supplied cash for immediate obligations, initially for two years at a high financing cost, secured by assets and paired with an interest of about 79.9%. It prevented near-term default but did not eliminate future collateral calls.

**Layer two: October 8 — a securities-lending facility of up to $37.8 billion.** It helped insurance subsidiaries meet cash demands from the unwinding of securities-lending transactions.

**Layer three: November 10 — Treasury purchases $40 billion of preferred shares under TARP.** The rescue is restructured. The Fed's original facility falls from $85 billion to $60 billion and receives revised maturity and pricing terms.

**Layer four: Maiden Lane II and III purchase assets and terminate contracts.**

```text
Maiden Lane II — resolving securities lending
New York Fed lends about $19.5 billion
                    │
                    ▼
SPV buys RMBS from AIG-related insurance subsidiaries
Market value about $20.8 billion; face value about $39.3 billion
                    │
                    ▼
AIG receives cash and ends the related securities-lending program

Maiden Lane III — resolving CDS exposure
New York Fed lends about $24.3 billion; AIG provides about $5 billion subordinated
                    │
                    ▼
SPV acquires CDOs with roughly $62.1 billion face value
Fair value at the time: about $29.6 billion
                    │
                    ▼
Counterparties sell the CDOs and retain collateral already paid by AIG
                    │
                    ▼
Related CDS contracts are terminated and future collateral calls stop
```

Maiden Lane III paid counterparties about **$26.8 billion in new cash**. They also retained about **$35.1 billion** of collateral previously posted by AIG. The protected CDO exposure was therefore settled at roughly par, prompting debate over whether large banks should have shared more of the loss. The $62.1 billion face value, $29.6 billion fair value, $24.3 billion Fed loan, and $26.8 billion cash payment describe different things. They do not mean that the government gave away another $62.1 billion. [New York Fed transaction details](https://www.newyorkfed.org/aboutthefed/aig/ml23.html)

Total AIG rescue commitments reached as much as approximately **$182.3 billion**, not cash spent at one time. After the government exited shares, warrants, and related arrangements in 2012–2013, Treasury and the Fed recorded a combined positive return of roughly **$22.7 billion**. That accounting return is not a measure of social benefit after unemployment and wider economic losses.

## 12 | Money-market funds and financial contagion

The Reserve Primary Fund held Lehman commercial paper. On September 16, it reduced its net asset value below $1 per share, or “broke the buck.” Investors rushed out of money-market funds, weakening the commercial-paper market on which companies relied for short-term financing.

The Fed responded with facilities including the **PDCF, TSLF, AMLF, and CPFF**, while dollar swap lines with other central banks eased the global dollar shortage. Most of these programs were market-liquidity tools, not government purchases of bank common or preferred shares, and should be distinguished from TARP.

## 13 | Goldman, Morgan Stanley, and the search for mergers and a new regulatory status

After Lehman's bankruptcy in September 2008, markets lost confidence in the short-term funding model used by the remaining independent investment banks. Officials and financial executives discussed possible combinations and capital partnerships. Morgan Stanley and JPMorgan were among the potential pairings, while Goldman Sachs and Citigroup also appeared in merger discussions at the time. None of these contacts produced a completed merger and they should not be treated as signed acquisition agreements.

The confirmed institutional change came on **September 21, 2008**, when the Fed approved Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley as bank holding companies. The change placed both groups under more direct Fed supervision and allowed them to adjust their funding and banking structures. It did not merge either company with another bank, nor did it mean that they had been entirely unable to use Fed emergency facilities before conversion; eligible broker-dealers already had access to the PDCF.

Their survival prospects improved through a combination of regulatory conversion, private capital, central-bank liquidity, and later TARP investment.

## 14 | Private capital: Buffett backs Goldman; MUFG backs Morgan Stanley

**Goldman Sachs and Berkshire Hathaway, September 23:** Berkshire bought **$5 billion** of perpetual preferred shares paying a **10% annual dividend**, plus warrants to buy $5 billion of common shares at **$115 per share**. Goldman would pay a premium to redeem the preferred shares and raised additional capital from the market. The deal does not show that Buffett knew in advance that Lehman would fail. Goldman and Lehman presented different terms and risks.

**Morgan Stanley and MUFG — intention September 22, agreement September 29, completion October 13:** Mitsubishi UFJ Financial Group invested **$9 billion** for an approximately **21% fully diluted interest**. At closing, the structure was revised to about **$7.8 billion of convertible preferred shares** and **$1.2 billion of non-convertible preferred shares**, each paying a 10% dividend. The transaction also established a long-term strategic relationship.

Both Goldman and Morgan Stanley later received **$10 billion** through the CPP. Private investment alone did not constitute their entire stabilization package.

## 15 | The major institutions: acquisitions and outcomes

**Bear Stearns → JPMorgan Chase:** a rescue acquisition announced and completed in March 2008.  
**Lehman Brothers → bankruptcy and sale by parts:** the holding company filed on September 15; Barclays acquired selected North American operations, while Nomura and others acquired parts of the Asian and European businesses.  
**Merrill Lynch → Bank of America:** announced September 15, completed January 1, 2009.  
**Washington Mutual Bank → FDIC seizure and transfer of operations to JPMorgan:** September 25.  
**Wachovia → Wells Fargo:** agreed in autumn 2008 and completed by year-end.  
**Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley:** remained independent groups, became bank holding companies, raised private capital, and accepted TARP funds.  
**Citigroup and Bank of America:** received additional support after their initial CPP investments as capital and troubled-asset pressures persisted.  
**Wells Fargo and JPMorgan:** grew through acquisitions but also accepted initial TARP capital. Receipt of CPP funds did not mean that every participating bank was near failure.  
**AIG:** sold assets and restructured over several years after the government rescue. It did not enter a Lehman-style bankruptcy and continues as an insurance group.

## 16 | TARP: why the plan moved from troubled assets to preferred shares

TARP originally contemplated buying troubled bank assets. Pricing them quickly was difficult: a low public purchase price could force larger write-downs, while an inflated price could shift losses to taxpayers. With markets already impaired, officials concluded that buying bank preferred shares could add loss-absorbing capital faster.

The first bill failed in the House on September 29. Congress authorized a $700 billion TARP on October 3. Paulson and others met the heads of nine large banks on October 13. On October 14, Treasury announced the **Capital Purchase Program**, initially allocating up to $250 billion, including $125 billion approved for the first nine institutions.

Standard CPP preferred shares paid a **5% dividend for five years** and **9% thereafter**, and came with warrants and compensation restrictions. This was an investment, not a free payment. It also did not guarantee an immediate increase in lending.

## 18 | Why did banks need other support after receiving TARP capital?

**Liquidity and capital solve different problems.** A liquidity shortage means a firm owes cash tomorrow and cannot readily obtain it. A capital shortage means its equity cushion may not absorb prospective losses. Fed secured lending, emergency market facilities, and Treasury preferred-share purchases addressed different failures.

Suppose a bank has $100 billion in assets, $95 billion in liabilities, and $5 billion in equity. A qualifying $10 billion preferred-share investment raises assets to $110 billion and equity to $15 billion in a simplified example. The larger cushion can absorb losses. It does not create qualified borrowers or ensure that the bank will take additional credit risk.

Citigroup first received $25 billion through CPP, then obtained another $20 billion and other arrangements as asset and confidence problems persisted. BofA received an initial $15 billion, another $10 billion associated with the Merrill transaction, and later $20 billion of additional support. Acquirers grew larger but also inherited troubled assets and risk.

## 20 | Twelve facts that are easy to confuse

1. **JPMorgan acquired Bear Stearns in a rescue transaction.** The Fed supported the resolution of specified assets through Maiden Lane; its loan was not JPMorgan equity.
2. **Buffett discussed an investment with Lehman.** Important terms and due-diligence concerns remained, so the financing was never assured.
3. **KDB's negotiations with Lehman failed.** The parties differed over price, asset risk, and transaction terms.
4. **Officials and bankers negotiated over Lehman.** The process involved several meetings, calls, and potential transaction structures.
5. **Barclays did not acquire Lehman as a whole.** U.K. approval, interim guarantees, and risk sharing remained unresolved.
6. **The SEC helped press Lehman toward Chapter 11.** It did not issue an administrative order declaring the holding company bankrupt.
7. **The Fed announced the AIG rescue after Lehman's bankruptcy.** AIG's CDS and securities-lending pressures had developed well before that day.
8. **AIG's exposure was cross-border and systemic.** It involved credit protection, securities lending, and short-term funding, not only ordinary insurance claims.
9. **Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley did not temporarily merge.** They became bank holding companies and raised private capital separately.
10. **Buffett invested in Goldman and MUFG in Morgan Stanley.** Both firms later also participated in TARP's capital program.
11. **The first nine institutions were approved for $125 billion of CPP capital.** Approval and disbursement occurred on different dates; the entire amount did not arrive on the day of the meeting.
12. **AIG's rescue did not end with the $85 billion facility.** Maiden Lane II and III, TARP capital, and counterparty settlements were also central to the resolution.

## 21 | After 2009: a stable financial system did not mean an immediate economic recovery

The NBER dates the U.S. recession from December 2007 through June 2009. The annual unemployment rate averaged about 9.3% in 2009 and 9.6% in 2010. Even as financial institutions regained capital and liquidity, households carried heavy mortgage burdens, businesses cut investment, and consumption and trade remained weak.

The Fed's 2009 SCAP stress test examined 19 large bank holding companies and made potential capital shortfalls under an adverse economy more transparent. The Fed also used rate cuts, secured lending, market facilities, and asset purchases to stabilize financial conditions. Dodd–Frank in 2010 sought to reform the resolution of large firms, derivatives oversight, and the supervision of systemically important institutions.

## 22 | What the crisis teaches about finance

**First:** a bank failure often begins when funding markets refuse to roll loans, before it appears in the income statement.  
**Second:** a large balance sheet is not the same as ample shareholder capital or immediately available cash.  
**Third:** derivatives can transfer credit exposure while concentrating new counterparty risk.  
**Fourth:** short-term funding, collateral calls, and forced asset sales can create a downward spiral.  
**Fifth:** a rescue decision depends not only on systemic importance, but also on legal authority, collateral, transaction feasibility, and politics.  
**Sixth:** an acquisition can save one bank while making the survivor larger and concentrating risk.  
**Seventh:** preventing financial collapse does not erase the cost to jobs, households, and the wider economy.

## 23 | Conclusion: what the crisis teaches about systemic risk and public rescue

Lehman had businesses worth buying. AIG did not qualify for rescue merely because it sold insurance. Goldman and Morgan Stanley were not stabilized by private billionaires alone. The nine banks did not all accept CPP capital because each was about to fail.

The central failure of 2008 was a sudden breakdown in short-term funding caused by deteriorating assets, excessive leverage, and distrust among financial institutions. Governments had to choose among market discipline, taxpayer protection, and the risk of a global financial collapse, with no clean option available.

The historical record shows that every rescue involved collateral, preferred shares, dividends, warrants, shareholder approval, cross-border regulation, bankruptcy procedure, and market confidence. Different instruments and funding arrangements cannot all be reduced to the claim that “the government printed money for banks.”
