> Data cutoff: September 16, 2026, 15:00 Asia/Taipei. This report uses only information available before the cutoff and excludes that evening's US retail sales, Federal Reserve decision and subsequent market reaction.

## What Is Changing

The AI investment theme is shifting from whether capital spending is growing to who can convert that spending into cash flow at a reasonable funding cost. TSMC's August revenue reached NT$514.806 billion, up 53.3% year on year, showing that chip demand remained strong. At the same time, the US ten-year Treasury yield reached 5.041% and WTI rose to approximately $105.84, lifting capital costs and inflation risk together.

Demand and rates are not mutually exclusive. AI orders can keep growing while equity valuations fall as discount rates rise. Revenue can also grow while free cash flow fails to improve if receivables, inventory, funding costs and depreciation rise faster. Cash conversion and credit-bearing capacity will create the next stage of differentiation.

## Why Now

At the cutoff, markets assigned an approximately 92.4% probability to a 25-basis-point Fed hike. Two-, ten- and thirty-year Treasury yields ended the prior session near 4.671%, 5.002% and 5.367%, respectively. Projects that depended on distant terminal values must now be recalculated with a higher risk-free rate, credit spread and equity risk premium.

The AI capital chain is also expanding rapidly. Wafers, advanced packaging, servers, networking, cooling, power, land and data centers all require capital before end revenue arrives, and each monetizes on a different schedule. Higher rates amplify the maturity mismatch between paying first and validating demand later.

## End Demand

End demand includes model training, inference, cloud services, enterprise software, automation, search, advertising, government and sovereign compute. Training demand is concentrated and compute-intensive but exposed to changes in model architecture and chip generations. Inference demand can be more persistent, but unit pricing, utilization and competition matter more.

Verifiable demand indicators include cloud revenue and utilization, paid inference volume, revenue per unit of compute, customer retention, GPU and ASIC shipments, advanced-packaging utilization, server shipments, and energized and actually utilized data-center IT load. Announced capex or megawatts are not end demand by themselves.

## Business Model

Semiconductor suppliers recover research and equipment investment through chip and wafer revenue. Server and networking suppliers generate cash through hardware margins and working-capital velocity. Cloud providers monetize rented compute, software and platform services. Data centers recover land, building and electrical investment through power, space and long leases. GPU-as-a-service providers depend on equipment utilization and rental income to recover short-lived assets.

Closer to upstream chips, demand visibility and pricing power can be greater, but customer concentration and cyclicality are higher. Infrastructure can produce steadier contracted cash flow, but adds land, power, financing and construction risk. Compute reselling can grow quickly but is most dependent on utilization, residual values and recurring funding.

## Who Pays

Ultimate payers are hyperscale cloud providers, AI laboratories, enterprises, governments, advertisers and consumers. A prominent brand in a project announcement does not establish that the contracting entity has the same credit quality or that end users have committed to pay.

High-quality demand includes minimum purchases, prepayments, take-or-pay provisions, long contracts, parent guarantees or verifiable usage. If the same requirement is recorded as committed by the chip buyer, cloud provider, compute reseller and data-center owner, the market may be counting the same demand more than once.

## Who Borrows

Borrowers include chip manufacturers, server and component suppliers, hyperscalers, data-center developers, utilities, telecommunications companies, REITs and special-purpose vehicles holding GPUs. Funding can come from corporate bonds, bank loans, project finance, leases, supplier finance, private credit or equity.

A technology order can transfer credit risk to another balance sheet. A data center may borrow before construction, a utility may build substations before demand arrives, and a special-purpose vehicle may borrow to buy GPUs. All ultimately depend on cloud or enterprise usage. Analysis must identify recourse, guarantees and who absorbs the first loss if demand is delayed.

## Capital Structure

A sound structure matches asset life, contract duration and debt maturity. Fabs and data-center buildings can use long-dated funding. GPUs, networking and cooling equipment require faster amortization or stronger residual-value protection. Fixed-rate debt, phased capex, conservative leverage, sufficient interest coverage and firm customer commitments reduce refinancing risk.

A weak structure finances long construction or rapidly depreciating equipment with short-dated floating-rate debt while assuming utilization, rents, valuations and rate cuts improve together. Supplier finance, circular prepayments, cross-holdings and unconsolidated special-purpose vehicles can also understate economic leverage.

## Credit Structure

The credit chain is: higher rates increase construction and equipment funding costs; project delays defer rent commencement or compute revenue; utilization below underwriting reduces cash flow; lower equipment residual values and asset valuations raise loan-to-value; and the borrower must add collateral, raise equity, refinance or default.

At the cutoff, VIX was only 17.20 and did not indicate broad market panic. The current pressure was therefore primarily a risk-free-rate and valuation shock, not a confirmed systemic credit event. A simultaneous deterioration in high-yield spreads, private-credit terms and project-loan conditions would show that stress was moving from equity into credit.

## Bull Case

The bull case is sustained growth in AI usage and cloud revenue, broader revenue gains across TSMC and the server supply chain, and data-center pre-leasing and actual load rising together. If long yields fall and credit spreads remain stable, companies can fund investment with operating cash flow and long-dated fixed-rate debt.

The beneficiary order should begin with chips and advanced packaging that have pricing power and fast cash conversion, followed by servers, networking, thermal and power equipment, and only then development projects without power, pre-leasing or current cash flow. The bull case is complete only when equity gains accompany improving free cash flow and interest coverage.

## Bear Case

The bear case is not that AI demand abruptly falls to zero. It is that high rates persist, energy and power costs rise, and project utilization remains below expectations. Companies may continue to increase capex while free cash flow deteriorates, eventually forcing them to reduce investment, delay energization or renegotiate contracts.

If hyperscalers cut capex guidance, GPU rental rates and equipment residual values fall, and long yields and credit spreads rise together, stress will spread through data centers, special-purpose vehicles, supplier receivables and bank loans.

## Weakest Link

The weakest projects have committed capital to land, construction or GPUs using short-dated floating-rate debt without reliable power, long-term customers and complete financing. The second weak point is suppliers with high customer concentration, rapidly rising receivables and slower inventory turnover.

The third weak point is applying a building's long life to short-lived equipment such as GPUs. If technology generations turn faster than debt amortizes, insufficient residual value directly weakens collateral quality.

## Leading Indicators

1. Two-, ten- and thirty-year Treasury yields, and whether the curve remains inverted or bear-steepens.
2. Investment-grade and high-yield spreads, private-credit terms, project-finance spreads and bank lending standards.
3. Hyperscaler capex, cloud revenue, utilization and free cash flow.
4. TSMC monthly revenue, advanced-packaging utilization, GPU and ASIC shipments, and AI server shipments.
5. Supplier receivables, inventory, cash-conversion cycles, interest coverage and capex commitments.
6. Data-center megawatts separated into power-secured, pre-leased, energized and actually utilized stages.
7. GPU rental rates, equipment residual values, secondary-market prices and special-purpose vehicle refinancing terms.
8. Power prices, substation and transmission completion, and the ability of long-term contracts to pass through energy costs.

## What the Market May Be Missing

The first blind spot is treating strong revenue as evidence that every AI asset can sustain a high valuation. TSMC revenue growth proves that some end demand exists. It does not establish equal utilization or credit quality for every data center, GPU or compute-reselling platform.

The second blind spot is that credit risk may not remain inside technology companies. Utilities, REITs, banks, private-credit funds and equipment lessors can supply capital first, leaving ratepayers, creditors or shareholders to absorb the risk. Investors must separate order beneficiaries from the ultimate credit bearers.

## Next Confirmation

The first tests are post-cutoff US retail sales and the Fed decision: whether two- and ten-year yields rise together, whether the dollar strengthens, and whether credit spreads follow. The second is whether subsequent monthly revenue growth remains broad across TSMC, servers and advanced packaging.

The third is the next round of hyperscaler capex, cloud growth and free-cash-flow guidance. Rising capex and utilization alongside stable credit conditions would validate the trend. Capex growth accompanied by weaker free cash flow, utilization and funding terms would require markets to price overbuilding.
